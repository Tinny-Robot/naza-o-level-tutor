## Kinematics: Projectile Motion

A projectile is any object thrown or projected into the air that is subject only to the acceleration of gravity. In projectile motion, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The horizontal velocity remains constant throughout the flight ($u_x = u \cos 	heta$) because there is no horizontal acceleration ($a_x = 0$). The vertical motion is subject to a constant downward acceleration due to gravity ($a_y = -g$). The vertical velocity at any time $t$ is given by $u_y = u \sin 	heta - gt$. The three key formulas for a projectile launched from ground level at an angle $	heta$ with speed $u$ are:
1. Time of flight ($T$): The total time the projectile spends in the air, given by $T = \frac{2u \sin 	heta}{g}$.
2. Maximum height ($H$): The maximum vertical displacement achieved, given by $H = \frac{u^2 \sin^2 	heta}{2g}$.
3. Horizontal Range ($R$): The total horizontal distance traveled, given by $R = \frac{u^2 \sin 2	heta}{g}$. The range is maximum when $	heta = 45^\circ$.

## Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force

Ohm's law states that the current ($I$) flowing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference ($V$) across its ends, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant ($V = IR$). The Electromotive Force (e.m.f., denoted by $E$) of a cell is the total work done in driving a unit charge round a complete circuit, including the cell itself. The potential difference across the external circuit is the terminal voltage ($V$). When a cell of e.m.f. $E$ and internal resistance $r$ is connected to an external resistor $R$, the current flowing in the circuit is $I = \frac{E}{R + r}$. The relationship can be rewritten as $E = V + Ir$, where $Ir$ represents the 'lost volts' due to the cell's internal resistance.

## Radioactive Decay and Half-life

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei with the emission of ionizing radiation ($lpha$-particles, $eta$-particles, or $\gamma$-rays). The rate of decay is proportional to the number of unstable nuclei ($N$) present at that instant: $-\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N$, where $\lambda$ is the decay constant. Integrating this yields the exponential decay law: $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, where $N_0$ is the initial number of nuclei. The half-life ($T_{1/2}$) is the time taken for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. By setting $N(t) = \frac{N_0}{2}$ and solving, we obtain the relationship: $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$.
