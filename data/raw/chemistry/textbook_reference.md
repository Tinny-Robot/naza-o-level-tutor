## Stoichiometry and the Mole Concept

The mole is the SI unit for the amount of substance. One mole of any substance contains exactly $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ elementary particles (Avogadro's constant). For gases at Standard Temperature and Pressure (s.t.p., which is $0^\circ\text{C}$ or $273\text{ K}$ and 1 atm or $760\text{ mmHg}$), one mole of any gas occupies a volume of $22.4\text{ dm}^3$ (molar volume). The number of moles ($n$) is related to mass ($m$) and molar mass ($M$) by $n = \frac{m}{M}$. For solutions, concentration in mol/dm³ ($C$) is given by $C = \frac{n}{V}$, where $V$ is volume in dm³. Empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound, whereas the molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule. The two are related by: Molecular Formula = (Empirical Formula)$_n$, where $n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass of Compound}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$.

## Redox Reactions and Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

Oxidation is defined as the loss of electrons or increase in oxidation number, while reduction is the gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation number. An oxidizing agent accepts electrons and is reduced, while a reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidized. Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte by passing an electric current through it. Faraday's first law of electrolysis states that the mass ($m$) of a substance discharged or deposited at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the electrolyte: $m = zQ = zIt$, where $I$ is current in amperes, $t$ is time in seconds, and $z$ is the electrochemical equivalent. Faraday's second law states that when the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the masses of the substances liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalents (molar mass divided by the charge of the ion). One Faraday ($1\text{ F}$) is the charge of one mole of electrons, which is equal to approximately $96500\text{ C}$.

## Organic Nomenclature and Saponification

Organic compounds are named systematically using the IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) rules. The name consists of a prefix (specifying substituents), a root word (specifying the number of carbon atoms in the longest continuous chain: meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-), and a suffix (specifying the principal functional group: -ane for alkanes, -ene for alkenes, -ol for alcohols, -oic acid for carboxylic acids, and -oate for esters). Saponification is the alkaline hydrolysis of fats or oils (which are glyceryl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids) using a strong base like sodium hydroxide ($NaOH$) or potassium hydroxide ($KOH$) to produce glycerol and soap (sodium or potassium salts of fatty acids). The equation is: $\text{Fat/Oil} + 3NaOH \rightarrow \text{Glycerol} + 3\text{Soap}$.
