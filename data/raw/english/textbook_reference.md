## Grammatical Clauses and Syntactic Functions

A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a finite verb. Dependent or subordinate clauses cannot stand alone as complete sentences and are classified based on their functions. A Noun Clause performs the functions of a noun: it can be the subject of a verb ('What he said was true'), the object of a transitive verb ('She believes that we will pass'), or the object of a preposition ('Pay attention to what I am saying'). An Adjectival Clause (or relative clause) modifies a noun or pronoun and is introduced by relative pronouns like who, whom, whose, which, or that. For example, in 'The student who won the prize is diligent', the clause 'who won the prize' modifies the noun 'student'. An Adverbial Clause modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb, indicating time, place, manner, reason, condition, concession, or result. For example, 'We started when the rain stopped' (Adverbial clause of time modifying the verb 'started').

## Guidelines for Summary Writing

Summary writing in the WASSCE English examination is designed to test your ability to extract main ideas and present them concisely. A good summary must satisfy three core rules: (1) Accuracy: The summarized points must correspond exactly to the facts in the passage without personal opinions or external knowledge. (2) Brevity: Do not write unnecessary details, examples, comparisons, or redundant illustrations. Get straight to the point. (3) Sentence Structure: Every point must be written in a complete grammatical sentence. Starting a summary point with a phrase, participle, or infinitive (e.g., 'To build more classrooms') instead of a full clause will result in a heavy penalty or a zero score for that point. Use linkers to coordinate thoughts but keep sentences simple.

## Oral English: Stress and Pronunciation

English is a stress-timed language, where certain syllables are pronounced louder and longer than others. In multi-syllable words, primary stress is marked by a vertical superscript stroke ('). For disyllabic words, nouns and adjectives usually have the stress on the first syllable (e.g., 'PER-mit', 'RE-cord'), while verbs and prepositions have the stress on the second syllable (e.g., 'per-MIT', 're-CORD'). Emphatic stress is used to highlight a specific word in a sentence to signal contrast. For example, in the sentence 'Tunde BOUGHT a new car', the stress on 'BOUGHT' implies that Tunde did not borrow, steal, or rent the car, but specifically bought it. If the sentence is 'Tunde bought a NEW car', the stress implies that Tunde did not buy an old or used car.
